Diwali in a minute

A festival that turns the darkest night into the brightest

What

Diwali, also called Deepavali, means “a row of lamps”. It is the biggest celebration of the year across India and for Indian families worldwide, a little like Christmas and New Year rolled into one.

When

The date follows the moon, so it moves each year, always landing in October or November. The main night falls on the new moon, when the sky is at its darkest.

Why

Different communities tell different origin stories. The best known comes from the Ramayana, an epic poem more than two thousand years old. The next few minutes tell it.

The story behind the lamps

The Ramayana, told by lamplight

“Ramayana” means Rama’s journey. The poet Valmiki composed it in Sanskrit as 24,000 verses, and it has since been retold in hundreds of versions from India to Thailand and Indonesia.

How it travelledFor centuries, villages heard it as shadow theatre: painted leather puppets held against a cloth, lit from behind by a single oil lamp. That is how it is told here.

Chapter 1 · Ayodhya

A prince everyone loved

Rama (RAH-muh) is the eldest son of the king of Ayodhya: calm, fair, and the finest archer in the land. He wins the hand of Princess Sita (SEE-tah) by stringing a divine bow that no other suitor could even lift. The ageing king prepares to hand him the throne, and the whole city celebrates.

Chapter 2 · The exile

A promise that cost a kingdom

On the eve of the coronation, Queen Kaikeyi reminds the king of two wishes he once promised her. She asks for her own son, Bharata, to be crowned, and for Rama to be sent to the forest for fourteen years. Rama could refuse. He goes willingly, so that his father’s word is not broken. Sita and his brother Lakshmana (LUHKSH-muh-nuh) insist on going with him.

Worth knowingBharata is horrified and refuses the crown. He places Rama’s sandals on the throne and rules only as a caretaker until his brother returns.

Chapter 3 · The forest

The golden deer

For thirteen years the three live simply among the trees. Then Ravana (RAH-vuh-nuh), the ten-headed king of Lanka, hears of Sita. He sends a magical golden deer to draw the brothers away from their hut, and while they are gone he carries Sita off across the sea in his flying chariot.

Worth knowingRavana is no simple monster. He is a brilliant scholar and a mighty ruler, and his ten heads are often said to stand for his vast learning. What brings him down is pride.

Chapter 4 · The search

Hanuman’s leap

Searching for Sita, the brothers find allies in the vanaras, a forest people usually pictured as monkeys. Their greatest hero is Hanuman (HUH-noo-mahn), who has forgotten his own strength until an elder reminds him of it. He leaps the ocean in a single bound, finds Sita held in a garden in Lanka, and gives her Rama’s ring as proof that help is coming.

Worth knowingRavana’s guards catch Hanuman and set his tail alight. He uses it to set the city on fire, then leaps home with the news.

Chapter 5 · The crossing

A bridge of stones

An ocean still lies between Rama’s army and Lanka, so they build across it. Thousands of vanaras haul rocks and trees to the shore and lay a causeway to the island, stone by stone, in five days. Even a small squirrel is said to have helped, rolling in the sand and shaking the grains onto the bridge.

Worth knowingA chain of shallow sandbanks really does run between India and Sri Lanka. Maps call it Adam’s Bridge. In India it is Ram Setu, “Rama’s bridge”.

Chapter 6 · Lanka

The last arrow

The war is long and costly. Ravana’s own brother, Vibhishana, had begged him to return Sita and was banished for saying so; he now stands with Rama. In the final duel, every head Rama strikes from Ravana grows back, because a store of amrita (uhm-REE-tah), the nectar of immortality, lies in his navel and keeps him alive. Vibhishana tells Rama the secret. Rama aims a single arrow at the navel, the nectar dries up, and the king falls. Sita is free.

Worth knowingThis victory has its own festival, Dussehra, about twenty days before Diwali. Towering effigies of Ravana are set alight in parks and fairgrounds.

Chapter 7 · The homecoming

The night of lamps

The fourteen years are over. Rama, Sita and Lakshmana fly home to Ayodhya and arrive on a moonless night. To guide them in, the people set a small clay lamp, a diya (DEE-yah), at every door, window and rooftop, until the whole city glows. Rama is crowned at last.

That is DiwaliEvery year, hundreds of millions of homes repeat that welcome. The lamps say that light outlasts darkness, and that the people we love find their way home.

How it is celebrated

Five days, each with its own job

Names and customs vary by region, but most families follow a rhythm like this.

  1. DhanterasHonours Dhanvantari, physician of the gods and patron of Ayurveda, who rose from the churning ocean carrying the nectar of immortality, so families pray for good health.
  2. Choti DiwaliLittle DiwaliRangoli, patterns made from coloured powder or flowers, are laid at the doorstep. Sweets are made and the first lamps are lit.
  3. DiwaliThe main nightFamilies dress up, pray to Lakshmi, the goddess of prosperity, and light every lamp in the house. Then comes the feast, the sweets and the fireworks.
  4. PadwaFor many communities this is new year’s day. Businesses open fresh account books, and gifts pass between husbands and wives.
  5. Bhai DoojA day for brothers and sisters, who visit each other, share a meal and exchange gifts.

One festival, many stories

Rama’s return is not the only reason for the lamps

The same night carries different meanings across India. What they share is the idea of light winning over darkness, and knowledge over ignorance.

Sikhs

Celebrate Bandi Chhor Divas, the “day of liberation”. In 1619 Guru Hargobind walked out of prison and brought 52 captive princes out with him.

Jains

Mark the night their great teacher Mahavira attained nirvana, final release from the cycle of rebirth.

Bengal and the east

The night belongs to Kali, the fierce goddess who destroys evil. Her worship runs past midnight.

South India

The story is Krishna’s defeat of the demon Narakasura. It is celebrated at dawn, a day before the north.
